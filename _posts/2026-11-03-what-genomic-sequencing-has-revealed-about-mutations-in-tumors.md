What Genomic Sequencing Has Revealed About Mutations in Tumors

Cancer is one of the most complex diseases because it does not arise from a single cause or genetic change. Instead, cancer develops when cells accumulate genetic alterations that disrupt the normal processes controlling cell growth, division, and survival. For many years, scientists could identify only a limited number of these alterations. However, advances in genomic sequencing have allowed researchers to examine the DNA of tumors in much greater detail, revealing how cancer develops at the molecular level. Two landmark papers published in 2013, Cancer Genome Landscapes by Bert Vogelstein and colleagues in Science and Lessons from the Cancer Genome by Levi Garraway and Eric Lander in Cell, helped establish a new understanding of cancer genetics. Their research demonstrated that tumors contain many different mutations, but only some contribute directly to cancer development. These discoveries have helped scientists identify important cancer-related genes, understand how tumors evolve, and develop more targeted approaches to treatment.


Understanding Mutations and SNPs

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence of a cell. DNA contains the genetic instructions that regulate how cells function, grow, and reproduce. When these instructions are altered, the effects can range from having no noticeable impact to disrupting important cellular processes. One type of genetic variation is a single-nucleotide polymorphism, or SNP (pronounced "snip"). A SNP is a difference in a single DNA nucleotide that occurs among individuals in a population. For example, one person may have an A at a particular position in the genome, while another person has a G. SNPs are common in the human genome and are often harmless. However, some can influence an individual's susceptibility to certain diseases. It is important to distinguish inherited SNPs from somatic mutations, which develop in individual cells during a person's lifetime. Somatic mutations can accumulate as cells divide and are especially important in cancer because they may alter the genes responsible for controlling cell growth. Genomic sequencing allows scientists to identify these DNA changes by comparing the genetic material of tumor cells with normal cells. This comparison helps researchers distinguish inherited variations from mutations acquired during tumor development.


Driver Genes, Oncogenes, and Tumor Suppressor Genes

One of the most important discoveries from cancer genome sequencing is that not every mutation found in a tumor contributes to cancer development. Vogelstein and colleagues distinguished between two major categories of mutations: driver mutations and passenger mutations. Driver mutations provide cancer cells with a growth or survival advantage, allowing them to reproduce more successfully. Passenger mutations accumulate in tumor cells but do not directly contribute to cancer progression. A gene containing a mutation that contributes to cancer development is known as a driver gene. These genes are especially important because they can disrupt the normal processes that regulate cell growth, survival, and DNA repair. Vogelstein and colleagues estimated that a typical tumor contains approximately two to eight driver gene mutations, while many additional mutations may be passengers.


Driver genes can be divided into two major categories: oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes.

Oncogenes are genes that promote cell growth and division when activated inappropriately. Their normal versions, called proto-oncogenes, help regulate healthy cell growth. However, mutations that activate proto-oncogenes can cause cells to divide excessively or survive when they should not. For example, mutations in the KRAS gene can lead to continuous growth signaling, contributing to the development of several cancer types. Tumor suppressor genes perform the opposite function. They normally help prevent uncontrolled cell growth by regulating cell division, repairing DNA damage, or triggering the elimination of severely damaged cells. When tumor suppressor genes are inactivated, cells may lose important protections against cancer. The TP53 gene, which produces the p53 protein, is a well-known example because it helps cells respond to DNA damage and prevents the continued growth of cells with serious genetic abnormalities. Together, these discoveries show that cancer can develop when the normal balance between cell growth and cell regulation is disrupted.


What Sequencing Has Revealed About Tumor Development

Genomic sequencing has shown that cancer is not caused by one universal mutation. Instead, different tumors can develop through different combinations of genetic alterations, even when they originate in the same tissue. Vogelstein and colleagues described the cancer genome as having a landscape of "mountains" and "hills." Mountains represent genes that are mutated frequently across many tumors, while hills represent genes that are mutated less frequently. This pattern demonstrates that some genetic alterations are common across cancer types, while others are specific to particular tumors. The researchers also identified approximately 140 genes that could promote cancer development when altered. These genes were associated with important cellular processes, including cell growth, survival, and the maintenance of DNA integrity. Garraway and Lander expanded on these findings in Lessons from the Cancer Genome. They explained that cancer-related mutations affect more than just genes involved in cell division. Sequencing studies have also identified alterations in genes that regulate chromatin, RNA splicing, metabolism, and other processes that influence how cells function. These discoveries revealed that cancer is a disease of disrupted cellular systems, rather than simply a disease of uncontrolled cell division.


How These Discoveries Have Changed Cancer Treatment

Understanding the mutations responsible for cancer has helped researchers develop more precise treatment strategies. Traditional cancer treatments, such as chemotherapy, often target rapidly dividing cells. Although these treatments can be effective, they may also damage healthy cells that divide quickly. Genomic sequencing has made it possible to identify specific genetic alterations that contribute to tumor growth. Researchers can then develop targeted therapies that interfere with the proteins or pathways affected by those mutations. For example, some cancers contain mutations in the EGFR gene, which can cause abnormal growth signaling. In certain patients, medications that inhibit EGFR can help slow or stop tumor growth. However, tumors are genetically diverse, and different cells within the same tumor may contain different mutations. This genetic variation can allow some cancer cells to survive treatment and eventually contribute to resistance. Sequencing can help researchers identify these differences and better understand why some treatments work for certain patients but not others.


Conclusion

Genomic sequencing has transformed our understanding of cancer by revealing the genetic changes that contribute to tumor development. It has helped scientists distinguish between driver and passenger mutations, identify oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, and recognize the importance of cellular pathways in cancer progression. The landmark studies by Vogelstein and colleagues and by Garraway and Lander demonstrated that cancer is a genetically complex disease involving many different alterations. Their findings have provided a foundation for modern cancer research and the development of targeted therapies. Although many questions remain about how tumors evolve and resist treatment, genomic sequencing continues to provide valuable information that can help researchers develop more effective approaches to cancer diagnosis, prevention, and treatment.
